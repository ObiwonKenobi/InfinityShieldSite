Flock Camera Tracker

Flock Safety license plate reader cameras mapped in Cheyenne and the rest of Laramie County, counted from OpenStreetMap and tracked over time.

No data yet. The first snapshot is collected automatically and the charts appear here once it is in.

What the cameras record

A Flock license plate camera is a camera. It photographs every vehicle that passes, and the plate read is only the part that gets indexed first. Flock describes a "vehicle fingerprint" feature that also records the make, body type, color, decals, bumper stickers and roof racks, and whether a plate is missing or temporary, so a search can run on a description with no plate number (Flock’s own description). What else shows in an image depends on the camera’s angle and lighting.

Some Flock models are general video cameras, not plate readers. 404 Media reported in December 2025 that dozens of Flock’s Condor pan-tilt-zoom cameras, which can be set to zoom in on people, had live video streams and admin panels reachable from the open internet. The product line and its capabilities change, so treat this as a description of what has been reported, not a spec sheet.

Where the data goes

Each capture, meaning the plate, the vehicle’s attributes, an image, the time and the camera’s location, goes to Flock’s cloud. Flock’s design limits searching to authorized users: the police or business that owns the camera and whoever they share with. Access has not always stayed inside that line, though. Flock cameras have been found streaming to the open internet, and a report on a Washington Post investigation found officers still using their logins after leaving their jobs or while suspended.

Every read carries a time and a place, so searching one plate returns a trail of where that vehicle went and when. The people running those searches are the police who own the cameras. Critics, including the ACLU and EFF, argue that following people this way without a warrant is unconstitutional. The federal court in Norfolk that ruled on Flock this year did not decide that for searches of the database.

Cheyenne’s own retention and sharing settings were not in the coverage reviewed for this page. City council records, or a public records request to the police department, would settle it.

The evidence: law, misuse, inference, retention

The law. In January 2026 a federal judge in Virginia held that photographing passing vehicles in Norfolk is not a Fourth Amendment search. On whether searching the stored database needs a warrant, the court did not reach the question: it found the plaintiffs could not challenge that part because the city had never searched for their vehicles. The plaintiffs are appealing, and the ACLU and EFF back them (WHRO; ACLU). It is one district court on one city’s facts, not a rule for Wyoming. State laws differ: Flock says it disabled its national lookup in states such as California that bar sharing across state lines (Stateline).

Misuse. A Washington Post analysis, reprinted by the Alaska Dispatch News, found at least 50 officers charged or accused of misusing license plate readers, and 26 of them used Flock’s cameras to spy on current or former partners or people they hoped to meet. In September 2026 prosecutors charged five Indianapolis police officers with misusing access to Flock cameras. EFF’s audit-log analysis, covering more than 12 million searches by over 3,900 agencies, found more than 50 agencies with searches tied to protest activity, often with only “protest” typed as the reason. Flock’s own post reads the same review as 19 of about 3,900 agencies, so the count depends on what is measured. Flock calls misuse unacceptable and says it will require an audit tool and case codes on searches by year end.

Gaps between cameras. The road network and the time stamps limit where a vehicle could have been between two reads, and four time-and-place points were enough to identify 95% of 1.5 million mobile phone users (de Montjoye et al., 2013). That study used phone data, not plate readers, and no study of route inference between plate-reader cameras was found. Flock argues its cameras cannot track the whole of anyone’s movements. A plate belongs to a registered owner, and most people commute alone, so a trail usually describes one person, though shared household cars, rentals and fleet vehicles can break that link.

Sharing and retention. Flock describes sharing one-to-one, within a radius or across a state, plus a national lookup for one full plate number. It now recommends seven days of retention, down from a 30-day default, and the change does not automatically apply to existing customers.

How to read this

These are cameras volunteers have mapped, not an official inventory. A jump in the line usually means someone surveyed an area, so it tracks installation but does not measure it, and cameras that were never mapped are missing. Counts include only nodes tagged as Flock Safety by manufacturer or brand; the fourth tile counts every plate reader tagged in the box, whatever the maker.

Both areas are rectangles (south, west, north, east), not legal boundaries, so the county box takes in a sliver of neighbouring counties on its north and west edges. Laramie County (bounding box): 40.99, -105.28, 41.6, -104.05; Cheyenne area (bounding box): 41.07, -104.95, 41.24, -104.65. Earlier snapshots marked attic are rebuilt from OpenStreetMap edit history, so they show what was mapped by that date and nothing about the days before it was surveyed.
