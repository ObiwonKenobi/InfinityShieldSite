What the cameras record

A Flock license plate camera is a camera. It photographs every vehicle that passes, and the plate read is only the part that gets indexed first. Flock describes a "vehicle fingerprint" feature that also records the make, body type, color, decals, bumper stickers and roof racks, and whether a plate is missing or temporary, so a search can run on a description with no plate number (Flock’s own description). What else shows in an image depends on the camera’s angle and lighting.

Some Flock models are general video cameras, not plate readers. 404 Media reported in December 2025 that dozens of Flock’s Condor pan-tilt-zoom cameras, which can be set to zoom in on people, had live video streams and admin panels reachable from the open internet. The product line and its capabilities change, so treat this as a description of what has been reported, not a spec sheet.

Where the data goes

Each capture, meaning the plate read, the vehicle attributes, an image, the time and the camera’s location, is uploaded to Flock’s cloud and searched through Flock’s software. The public cannot search it. Access belongs to the agency or business that owns the camera and to whoever that owner shares with. Flock describes sharing one-to-one between departments, within a radius or across a state, plus a national lookup for one full plate number (Stateline, Aug 2026). Each owner sets its own sharing and retention. Flock now recommends seven days of retention, down from a 30-day default, and the change does not automatically apply to existing customers.

Every read carries a time and a place, so the reads for one plate form a trail. Each camera a vehicle passes adds a point, and a search on that plate returns where it was and when. The more cameras along someone’s routes, the denser the trail, and shared access carries it past one department’s cameras. Privacy groups including the ACLU and EFF argue that a searchable history like this lets someone reconstruct a person’s movements without a warrant. Flock argues its cameras cannot track the whole of anyone’s movements and treats misuse as isolated. The trail follows the vehicle, not the person, and it has gaps wherever there is no camera, which the map above shows.

The people with access are the exposure. Every search is logged with a stated reason, and Flock says it will require an audit tool by the end of the year. In September 2026, prosecutors charged five Indianapolis police officers with misusing access to Flock cameras. Cheyenne’s own retention and sharing settings were not in the coverage reviewed for this page. The city council’s records, or a public records request to the police department, would settle it.

How to read this

These are cameras volunteers have mapped, not an official inventory. A jump in the line usually means someone surveyed an area, so it tracks installation but does not measure it, and cameras that were never mapped are missing. Counts include only nodes tagged as Flock Safety by manufacturer or brand; the fourth tile counts every plate reader tagged in the box, whatever the maker.

Both areas are rectangles (south, west, north, east), not legal boundaries, so the county box takes in a sliver of neighbouring counties on its north and west edges. Laramie County (bounding box): 40.99, -105.28, 41.6, -104.05; Cheyenne area (bounding box): 41.07, -104.95, 41.24, -104.65. Earlier snapshots marked attic are rebuilt from OpenStreetMap edit history, so they show what was mapped by that date and nothing about the days before it was surveyed.
